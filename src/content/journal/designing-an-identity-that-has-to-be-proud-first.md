Some briefs are about making something look good. Leritaz Maronaz was never one of those. It's an illustrated book on the Maroons, the enslaved Mauritians who escaped captivity and resisted colonial rule rather than live under it, told chapter by chapter through real figures of that resistance. Louis T. Houat, the exiled abolitionist novelist, is one of them. That's not a brand you get to treat like a product launch.

## The rule I set before drawing anything

Before a single shape got drawn, I decided the identity couldn't be loud for the sake of being loud. It had to be *proud* first. That sounds like a slogan, but it changed real decisions: no stock-photo palm tree, no script font standing in for "heritage," none of the shortcuts that make a cultural identity read as a postcard instead of a people's actual history.

The mark grew out of hand-drawn texture instead of a grid: a bare footprint built from raffia-like strokes, paired with a bold, condensed logotype that reads as carved as much as typeset. Every shape is pushed slightly off-perfect on purpose. A project representing people, land and memory doesn't get to look machine-made.

## Why the process became part of the design

The illustration work underneath the mark runs the same way the mark does: ink linework built up into flat, warm colour by hand, panel by panel. At some point I stopped treating that as a production step to hide and started treating it as part of what the book is actually showing you. A case study, or a book page, that only shows the finished colour panel tells you less than one that lets you drag between the line art and the final piece and watch the work happen in front of you.

That's a genuinely small decision with an outsized effect: it turns "trust me, this was made carefully" into something you can see for yourself.

## The palette had to do real work too

Earth, fire and ocean: sun-bleached orange and red for daylight and confrontation, charcoal and ember for the scenes that happen at night. Not because that palette looks nice on a brand board, but because the two registers in the book, the daily life and the resistance, needed to read as different emotional weather without a single word of text. Colour was doing narrative work, not decoration.

## What I'd take into the next cultural project

Set the rule before you draw. "Proud, not loud" took five seconds to write down and shaped every choice after it, which is the whole point of a rule like that: it's not a tagline, it's a filter you run every decision through later, especially the ones you're tempted to rush.

Cultural work especially rewards this. Give me a museum, a heritage brand, a local story worth telling, and the first question I'm asking isn't "what does this look like," it's "what does this have to be careful about, and why does that matter more than the logo."
